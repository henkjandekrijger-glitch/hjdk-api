## Cars & Engines
Karl Benz's 1886 Patent-Motorwagen is widely considered the first practical automobile.
In 1888, Bertha Benz took the first long-distance car trip, about 106 kilometers, without telling her husband. She cleared a blocked fuel line with her hatpin along the way.
Bertha Benz bought fuel for that trip at a pharmacy in Wiesloch, Germany, which is now celebrated as the world's first filling station.
Henry Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to build a Model T from about 12 hours to about 90 minutes.
From 1914 to 1925, the Model T came only in black, partly because black paint dried fastest.
The Volkswagen Beetle sold more than 21 million units, one of the best-selling single designs in car history.
The Toyota Corolla is the best-selling car nameplate of all time, with more than 50 million sold.
The Ford F-Series has been America's best-selling pickup truck every year since 1977.
A Formula 1 car makes so much downforce that, in theory, it could drive upside down on the ceiling of a tunnel at high speed.
Formula 1 drivers can lose 2 to 4 kilograms in sweat during a hot race.
A Formula 1 team can change all four tires in under two seconds. The record is about 1.8 seconds.
The first Indianapolis 500 was held in 1911.
ThrustSSC set the land speed record of about 1,228 kilometers per hour in 1997 and became the first car to officially break the sound barrier.
Volvo engineer Nils Bohlin invented the three-point seat belt in 1959, and Volvo let every carmaker use the patent for free.
The name Motorola combines motor and Victrola, because the company's early hit was a radio for cars.
Cruise control was invented by Ralph Teetor, a blind engineer who was annoyed by his lawyer's jerky driving.
Lamborghini started out making tractors. According to the famous story, Ferruccio Lamborghini began building sports cars after a dispute with Enzo Ferrari.
Ferrari's prancing horse logo first appeared on the fighter plane of Italian World War I pilot Francesco Baracca.
Peugeot made pepper grinders before it made cars, and it still makes them today.
A typical car contains around 30,000 parts when you count every small piece.
Around the year 1900, roughly a third of the cars on American roads were electric.
The 1912 Cadillac was the first car with an electric starter, ending the dangerous hand crank.
A modern car can contain more than 100 million lines of software code.
The new car smell comes from chemicals evaporating from the plastics, glues and fabrics inside.
The word Jeep may come from GP, for general purpose, or from a magical creature called Eugene the Jeep in the Popeye comics.
Mercedes was named after Mercedes Jellinek, the daughter of an early car dealer and racer.
Toyota comes from the founding Toyoda family. The new spelling takes eight strokes in Japanese, considered a lucky number.
NASCAR traces its roots to Prohibition-era bootleggers who souped up their cars to outrun the police.
Rolls-Royce has said that about two thirds of all the cars it has ever built are still on the road.
The DeLorean car company was already bankrupt before Back to the Future made its car famous.

## Planes & Pilots
The Wright brothers' first flight in 1903 covered about 37 meters, less than the wingspan of a Boeing 747.
The Wright brothers flipped a coin to decide who would fly first. Wilbur won, but his attempt failed, so Orville made history days later.
Neither Wright brother ever married, and they paid for their experiments with money from their bicycle shop.
The Wright brothers built their own wind tunnel to test more than 200 wing shapes.
A piece of fabric from the Wright Flyer went to the Moon with Neil Armstrong in 1969.
A Boeing 747 is made of about 6 million parts.
An airplane's black box is actually bright orange, so it is easier to find after a crash.
On average, each commercial airliner is hit by lightning about once a year, and planes are built to handle it.
Pilots and copilots often eat different meals, so food poisoning cannot take out both of them.
The Concorde crossed from London to New York in about 3.5 hours. Its fastest crossing took under 3 hours.
The Concorde flew so high, about 18 kilometers up, that passengers could see the curve of the Earth.
Heat from friction made the Concorde stretch by 15 to 25 centimeters during supersonic flight.
The SR-71 Blackbird is the fastest jet-powered crewed aircraft ever, at more than 3,500 kilometers per hour.
The SR-71 leaked fuel on the ground, because its panels only sealed tight once they heated up in flight.
Chuck Yeager broke the sound barrier in 1947 while flying with two broken ribs from a horse-riding accident.
Charles Lindbergh flew solo from New York to Paris in 1927 in about 33.5 hours. A fuel tank blocked his front view, so he used a periscope.
Amelia Earhart became the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic in 1932.
Airplane windows are rounded because square corners concentrate stress. Early jets with square windows suffered disasters.
The tiny hole in an airplane window balances the air pressure between the window panes.
Cabin lights are dimmed for takeoff and landing so your eyes are already adjusted if you need to get out in the dark.
Jet engines are tested by firing dead chickens or gel birds into them at high speed, to make sure they survive bird strikes.
The Airbus A380 is the largest passenger airliner ever built and can seat more than 800 people.
The emergency call Mayday comes from the French m'aider, meaning help me.
Twin-engine airliners are certified to keep flying for hours on just one engine.
Contrails are clouds made of ice crystals that form from water vapor in jet exhaust.
The Red Baron, Manfred von Richthofen, was credited with 80 air victories in World War I.
Igor Sikorsky flew the first practical single-rotor helicopter, the VS-300, in 1939.
The Hughes H-4 Hercules, nicknamed the Spruce Goose, held the record for the largest wingspan for over 70 years, but flew only once, in 1947.
Despite its nickname, the Spruce Goose was built mostly of birch.
The longest nonstop passenger flights, such as Singapore to New York, last about 18 to 19 hours.

## Ships & the Sea
The Titanic carried lifeboats for only about half of the people on board.
The wreck of the Titanic lies about 3,800 meters deep and was found in 1985.
The distress signal SOS does not stand for anything. It was chosen because it is easy to send in Morse code.
The largest container ships are about 400 meters long, longer than the Eiffel Tower is tall.
One of today's biggest container ships can carry more than 24,000 containers.
Roughly 80 to 90 percent of world trade travels by sea.
In 2021, the giant container ship Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal for six days, holding up billions of dollars of trade every day.
The USS Constitution, launched in 1797, is the oldest commissioned warship still afloat.
It earned the nickname Old Ironsides in 1812, when British cannonballs seemed to bounce off its thick oak hull.
A knot is one nautical mile per hour. Sailors once measured speed by counting knots on a rope tied to a floating log.
The left side of a ship was once called larboard, but it sounded too much like starboard, so it became port.
Starboard comes from steer board, the steering oar old ships carried on their right side.
Pirates very rarely made anyone walk the plank. There are only a handful of recorded cases.
Blackbeard reportedly tied slow-burning fuses into his beard during battle so he would look terrifying in the smoke.
In 1872, the ship Mary Celeste was found drifting in the Atlantic in good condition, with nobody on board.
The Bermuda Triangle does not have more disappearances than other busy stretches of ocean, according to the US Navy and NOAA.
The USS Gerald R. Ford is the largest warship ever built, about 337 meters long.
An aircraft carrier's catapult can launch a jet from standstill to about 265 kilometers per hour in about two seconds.
A nuclear-powered aircraft carrier can sail for more than 20 years before it needs to refuel.
The Turtle, a one-man wooden submarine, was used in 1776 during the American Revolution, the first submarine used in war.
In 1960, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh reached the bottom of the Mariana Trench in the bathyscaphe Trieste.
Film director James Cameron made the first solo dive to the deepest point of the ocean in 2012.
Columbus did not prove the world was round. Educated Europeans had known that for nearly 2,000 years.
Magellan's expedition made the first trip around the world from 1519 to 1522, but Magellan himself was killed in the Philippines.
Only 18 of the roughly 270 men who set out with Magellan's fleet made it home on the first voyage around the world.
British Royal Navy sailors received a daily ration of rum until July 31, 1970, a day still known as Black Tot Day.
British sailors were nicknamed limeys because they were given lime juice to prevent scurvy.
Scurvy, caused by a lack of vitamin C, killed more sailors in the age of sail than storms and battles combined, by many estimates.
The Swedish warship Vasa sank in 1628 on its first voyage, about 1,300 meters from shore. It was raised in 1961 and is now a museum.
Every lighthouse has its own pattern of flashes, so sailors can tell which one they are looking at in the dark.

## Military History
At Thermopylae in 480 BC, the famous 300 Spartans fought alongside several thousand other Greek soldiers.
Roman legionaries marched about 30 kilometers a day carrying packs of 30 to 40 kilograms.
In 218 BC, the general Hannibal crossed the Alps to attack Rome with an army that included war elephants.
In 1986, the Isles of Scilly and the Netherlands signed a peace treaty to end a symbolic war that had technically lasted 335 years, without a single shot fired.
According to a famous story, Napoleon was once overrun by a horde of rabbits during a hunting party.
During World War II, the American Ghost Army fooled German forces with inflatable tanks, fake radio traffic and loudspeakers playing sounds of troops.
A Syrian brown bear named Wojtek served with the Polish army in World War II, helped carry ammunition crates and was given the rank of corporal.
A carrier pigeon named Cher Ami delivered a message in 1918 that helped save almost 200 trapped American soldiers.
Navajo code talkers used their own language to send messages in World War II, and the code was never broken.
Japanese soldier Hiroo Onoda kept fighting in the Philippine jungle until 1974, because he refused to believe the war had ended until his old commander came to relieve him.
On D-Day, June 6, 1944, about 156,000 Allied troops landed in Normandy.
The D in D-Day simply stands for day.
Alan Turing and the codebreakers at Bletchley Park cracked Germany's Enigma messages, building on earlier work by Polish mathematicians.
Winston Churchill escaped from a prisoner-of-war camp in South Africa in 1899, while working as a war reporter.
Churchill won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1953, not the Peace Prize.
George Washington's false teeth were made of ivory, metal and human and animal teeth, but never wood.
M&M's were created in 1941, and soldiers in World War II carried them because the candy shell kept the chocolate from melting.
Duct tape was developed during World War II to seal ammunition boxes that could be ripped open quickly.
Aviator sunglasses were developed for American pilots in the 1930s.
The trench coat got its name from the trenches of World War I, where officers wore it.
Wristwatches became popular with men during World War I, because checking a pocket watch in battle was impractical.
The Pentagon was built in just 16 months, from 1941 to 1943.
In the American Civil War, about two thirds of soldiers who died were killed by disease, not by battle.
In 1945, a Japanese balloon bomb killed six people on a picnic in Oregon, the only deaths from enemy action on the US mainland in World War II.
Genghis Khan's empire ran a relay system of horse riders and stations that could carry messages up to about 200 kilometers in a day.
The Spanish reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula, the Reconquista, lasted almost 800 years.
The Hundred Years' War ended in 1453, the same year the city of Constantinople fell.
Military medic Desmond Doss refused to carry a weapon, yet saved about 75 soldiers at Okinawa and received the Medal of Honor.
Military time, like 1300 hours for 1 PM, avoids confusion between morning and evening.
The salute may come from medieval knights raising their visors to show who they were.

## Explorers & Adventurers
Roald Amundsen reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911, about five weeks before Robert Falcon Scott.
Scott and his four companions died on the way back from the pole.
Ernest Shackleton's ship Endurance was crushed by Antarctic ice in 1915, yet all 28 men of the expedition survived.
To get help, Shackleton and five men sailed about 1,300 kilometers in a small open lifeboat through some of the roughest seas on Earth.
The wreck of the Endurance was found in 2022, about 3,000 meters deep and remarkably intact.
Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay were the first to reach the top of Mount Everest, on May 29, 1953.
Hillary later spent decades building schools and hospitals for Sherpa communities in Nepal.
In 1978, Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler reached the top of Everest without bottled oxygen, something doctors thought might be impossible.
Matthew Henson was part of the 1909 expedition that claimed to reach the North Pole, and he may have been the first to arrive.
Moroccan explorer Ibn Battuta traveled about 120,000 kilometers in the 1300s, far more than Marco Polo.
On the Lewis and Clark expedition of 1804 to 1806, only one member died, most likely of appendicitis.
In 1947, Thor Heyerdahl and five crewmates crossed about 6,900 kilometers of the Pacific on a balsa wood raft called Kon-Tiki.
Jacques Cousteau co-invented the Aqua-Lung, the first popular scuba system, in 1943.
Leif Erikson reached North America around the year 1000, about 500 years before Columbus.
Captain James Cook charted the coasts of New Zealand and eastern Australia in the 1770s.
Roald Amundsen was also the first to sail the Northwest Passage, between 1903 and 1906.
In 1889 and 1890, reporter Nellie Bly traveled around the world in 72 days, beating the fictional record of Around the World in Eighty Days.
In 2012, Felix Baumgartner jumped from about 39 kilometers up and broke the sound barrier in free fall.
Two years later, Google executive Alan Eustace jumped from about 41 kilometers, a new record.
More than 6,000 different people have now stood on the summit of Mount Everest.
The Seven Summits are the highest peaks on each continent. Dick Bass was the first to climb them all, in 1985.
The Pan-American Highway runs about 30,000 kilometers from Alaska to Argentina, broken only by the jungle of the Darién Gap.
In 1969, Robin Knox-Johnston became the first person to sail alone around the world without stopping. It took him 312 days.
Adventurer Bear Grylls climbed Everest at 23, less than two years after breaking his back in a parachuting accident.
Alexander von Humboldt has more species and places named after him than almost anyone else in history.
Explorer Henry Morton Stanley searched Africa for months to find missionary David Livingstone, and greeted him with the famous line "Dr. Livingstone, I presume?"
In 1931, Swiss scientist Auguste Piccard rode a sealed balloon gondola about 15.8 kilometers up, becoming one of the first two people to reach the stratosphere.
Polar explorers on the ice can burn more than 6,000 calories a day pulling their sleds.
In 1953, Hillary and Tenzing spent only about 15 minutes on the summit of Everest.
Fridtjof Nansen deliberately froze his ship Fram into the Arctic ice in 1893 to let the currents carry it across the polar sea.

## Toughest Men in History
British officer Jack Churchill fought in World War II armed with a longbow and a Scottish broadsword.
Adrian Carton de Wiart was shot in the face, skull, stomach, ankle, leg, hip and ear, lost an eye and a hand, and kept serving through two world wars.
In 1912, Theodore Roosevelt was shot in the chest before a speech and went on to speak for about 90 minutes before going to the hospital.
The bullet that hit Roosevelt was slowed by his folded 50-page speech and his metal glasses case.
President Andrew Jackson carried a bullet near his heart for decades after a duel.
In 1823, fur trapper Hugh Glass survived a grizzly bear attack and made his way about 300 kilometers back to safety, the story behind The Revenant.
Tsutomu Yamaguchi survived both atomic bombings, at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and lived to the age of 93.
Explorer Ranulph Fiennes cut off his own frostbitten fingertips with a saw in his garden shed.
Fiennes also ran seven marathons on seven continents in seven days in 2003, just months after a heart attack and bypass surgery.
In 2003, climber Aron Ralston freed himself after being trapped by a boulder for 127 hours by amputating his own arm.
In 1985, climber Joe Simpson survived a fall into a crevasse in the Andes with a shattered leg and crawled for days back to camp.
Wim Hof, nicknamed the Iceman, ran a half marathon barefoot above the Arctic Circle.
Ultramarathon runner Dean Karnazes ran 50 marathons in 50 US states in 50 days in 2006.
Karnazes once ran about 560 kilometers without stopping to sleep.
In 2016, strongman Eddie Hall became the first person to deadlift 500 kilograms.
In 2006, climber Lincoln Hall survived a night near the top of Everest after being left for dead.
Sailor Steve Callahan survived 76 days adrift in a life raft in the Atlantic in 1982.
During World War II, ship steward Poon Lim survived 133 days alone on a raft in the Atlantic.
Olympic runner Louis Zamperini survived 47 days on a raft in the Pacific in World War II, then more than two years as a prisoner of war.
Fisherman José Salvador Alvarenga drifted across the Pacific for about 14 months, from Mexico to the Marshall Islands, and survived.
In 2018, Ross Edgley swam about 2,880 kilometers around Great Britain, spending 157 days at sea.
In 2017, Alex Honnold climbed the 900-meter face of El Capitan in Yosemite without ropes, in under four hours.
According to legend, the ancient Greek wrestler Milo of Croton carried a calf on his shoulders every day until it was a full-grown bull.
Abebe Bikila won the 1960 Olympic marathon in Rome running barefoot.
At the 1964 Olympics, Abebe Bikila won the marathon again, just weeks after having his appendix removed.
In 1953, climber Hermann Buhl reached the summit of Nanga Parbat alone and survived a night standing on a tiny ledge at about 8,000 meters.
Fighter pilot Douglas Bader lost both legs in a crash in 1931 and still flew combat missions in World War II.
Bader was later shot down and captured, and tried to escape from prison camps so often that the Germans threatened to take away his artificial legs.
American runner Glenn Cunningham was badly burned in a schoolhouse fire as a boy, was told he might never walk, and later set the world record in the mile.
Polar explorer Douglas Mawson survived alone in Antarctica in 1913 after both his companions died, walking about 160 kilometers back to base.

## Sports Legends
The Boston Red Sox sold Babe Ruth to the New York Yankees in 1920 and then went 86 years without winning the World Series.
Before he became a home run king, Babe Ruth was one of the best pitchers in baseball.
Muhammad Ali was born Cassius Clay and won an Olympic gold medal in boxing in 1960.
In 1967, Ali was stripped of his title for refusing to be drafted into the army. He later won the heavyweight crown back.
Michael Jordan was not cut from his high school team. As a sophomore, he was placed on the junior varsity squad instead.
In 1994, Michael Jordan left basketball to play minor league baseball for the Birmingham Barons.
Wilt Chamberlain scored 100 points in a single NBA game in 1962.
Wayne Gretzky had so many assists that even without a single goal, he would still be the NHL's all-time leading scorer.
Pelé is credited with more than 1,000 goals in his career when friendly matches are included.
Jesse Owens won four gold medals at the 1936 Olympics in Berlin.
Jackie Robinson broke Major League Baseball's color barrier in 1947. Every player now wears his number 42 on April 15.
Lou Gehrig played 2,130 games in a row, a record that stood for 56 years until Cal Ripken Jr. broke it in 1995.
Tom Brady won seven Super Bowls, more than any single NFL franchise.
Tiger Woods held all four major golf titles at the same time in 2000 and 2001.
Jack Nicklaus won 18 major golf championships, still the record.
At the 1968 Olympics, Bob Beamon long-jumped 8.90 meters, beating the old world record by 55 centimeters.
Roger Bannister ran the first mile under four minutes in 1954 while training to be a doctor.
Bannister's mile record lasted only 46 days.
At the 1980 Winter Olympics, a team of American college players beat the mighty Soviet Union in what became known as the Miracle on Ice.
Joe DiMaggio got a hit in 56 straight games in 1941, a Major League record that still stands.
Cy Young won 511 games as a pitcher. No modern pitcher has come anywhere close.
Nolan Ryan threw seven no-hitters and struck out 5,714 batters, both Major League records.
Rocky Marciano retired as the undefeated heavyweight champion, with a record of 49 wins and no losses.
Mike Tyson became the youngest heavyweight champion in history in 1986, at the age of 20.
Kareem Abdul-Jabbar was the NBA's all-time top scorer for 39 years, until LeBron James passed him in 2023.
In the same 1986 World Cup match against England, Diego Maradona scored the Hand of God goal and, four minutes later, the Goal of the Century.
As a boy, Lionel Messi was diagnosed with a growth hormone deficiency, and Barcelona paid for his treatment.
Cristiano Ronaldo is the all-time top goal scorer in men's international football.
Gordie Howe played professional hockey in six different decades.
Golfer Ben Hogan won six major championships after a near-fatal head-on collision with a bus in 1949.

## Space & Astronauts
Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on April 12, 1961.
Alan Shepard, the first American in space, flew for just 15 minutes in 1961.
Buzz Aldrin used a felt-tip pen to push in a broken circuit breaker so Apollo 11 could leave the Moon.
The Apollo 11 astronauts could not get life insurance, so they signed hundreds of envelopes their families could sell if they did not come back.
After returning from the Moon, the Apollo 11 crew filled out a US customs form listing Moon rocks and Moon dust.
When an oxygen tank exploded on Apollo 13, the crew survived by using their lunar module as a lifeboat.
Apollo astronauts left 96 bags of human waste on the Moon.
Astronauts who walked on the Moon said moon dust smelled like spent gunpowder.
Three lunar rovers driven by Apollo astronauts are still parked on the Moon.
Gene Cernan, the last person to walk on the Moon in 1972, wrote his daughter's initials in the lunar dust.
Without exercise, astronauts can lose about 1 percent of their bone mass for every month in space.
Astronauts on the International Space Station drink water recycled from their own sweat and urine.
The International Space Station is about as long as an American football field.
People have lived on the International Space Station without a break since November 2000.
In space, gas and liquid do not separate in your stomach, so astronauts cannot burp normally.
Astronauts sleep in sleeping bags strapped to the wall so they do not drift around.
A NASA spacesuit for spacewalks costs millions of dollars to build.
Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space in 1963, flying alone for almost three days.
Soviet cosmonauts carried a special survival gun for decades, in case they landed in remote forests with bears and wolves.
In 2024, SpaceX caught a returning Super Heavy rocket booster in mid-air with giant mechanical arms nicknamed chopsticks.
Some SpaceX Falcon 9 boosters have flown and landed more than 20 times.
The golden records on the Voyager probes carry greetings in 55 languages, sounds of Earth and music, including Chuck Berry.
A day on Mars is called a sol and lasts about 24 hours and 39 minutes.
In 2021, NASA's Ingenuity became the first aircraft to make a powered flight on another planet.
Ingenuity carried a tiny piece of fabric from the Wright brothers' 1903 Flyer.
Astronaut Scott Kelly spent almost a year in space while his identical twin brother Mark stayed on Earth, so NASA could compare them.
Russian cosmonaut Valeri Polyakov holds the record for the longest single stay in space, about 437 days.
The International Space Station is often the third brightest object in the night sky, after the Moon and Venus.
Neil Armstrong's spacesuit was made by a company better known for making bras and girdles, Playtex.
The Saturn V rocket that carried astronauts to the Moon was as tall as a 36-story building.

## Engineering Marvels
The Great Pyramid of Giza is made of about 2.3 million stone blocks.
The base of the Great Pyramid is level to within about 2 centimeters across 230 meters.
The Hoover Dam contains enough concrete to pave a two-lane highway from San Francisco to New York.
The Golden Gate Bridge is painted International Orange, a color chosen to stand out in San Francisco's fog.
The main cables of the Golden Gate Bridge contain about 129,000 kilometers of wire, enough to wrap around the Earth three times.
The Panama Canal lifts ships about 26 meters with locks, and a crossing takes about 8 to 10 hours.
The Channel Tunnel between England and France has about 38 kilometers under the sea, the longest undersea section of any tunnel.
The Gotthard Base Tunnel in Switzerland is the longest railway tunnel in the world, at about 57 kilometers.
The Empire State Building went up in about 410 days, in 1930 and 1931.
The Eiffel Tower was the tallest structure in the world for 41 years, until the Chrysler Building passed it in 1930.
The Chrysler Building's spire was secretly built inside the tower and raised into place in about 90 minutes to beat a rival skyscraper.
The Colosseum in Rome could hold about 50,000 spectators and had a huge retractable awning for shade.
Some Roman harbor concrete has survived 2,000 years in seawater, and actually got stronger over time.
The dome of the Pantheon in Rome, built nearly 2,000 years ago, is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world.
The Three Gorges Dam in China is the largest power station in the world.
NASA calculated that the water held behind the Three Gorges Dam lengthens Earth's day by about 0.06 millionths of a second.
The Large Hadron Collider is a 27-kilometer ring kept colder than outer space.
The Danyang-Kunshan Grand Bridge in China is the longest bridge in the world, about 164 kilometers long.
The 1915 Çanakkale Bridge in Turkey has the longest main span of any suspension bridge, over 2,000 meters.
Taipei 101 has a 660-tonne steel ball hanging inside it to reduce swaying in typhoons and earthquakes.
In 1884, showman P.T. Barnum led 21 elephants across the Brooklyn Bridge to prove it was safe.
The iron skeleton inside the Statue of Liberty was designed by Gustave Eiffel.
The Trans-Alaska Pipeline zigzags across the land so it can flex with temperature changes and earthquakes.
At Machu Picchu, the stones fit together so tightly without mortar that a knife blade cannot slide between them.
The tallest pier of the Millau Viaduct in France is taller than the Eiffel Tower.
The Palm Jumeirah island in Dubai was built from about 94 million cubic meters of sand.
The Dutch Delta Works, built to protect the Netherlands from the sea, has been named one of the seven wonders of the modern world by American civil engineers.
The Burj Khalifa's fastest elevators climb at about 10 meters per second.
The Hoover Dam was finished in 1936, two years ahead of schedule.
The Great Wall of China was built and rebuilt over about 2,000 years by many different dynasties.

## Inventors & Big Ideas
Nikola Tesla's alternating current system beat Thomas Edison's direct current to power the world's electric grids.
Tesla worked for Edison as a young man and quit over a bonus he said he was promised but never paid.
Thomas Edison held 1,093 US patents.
Alexander Graham Bell suggested answering the telephone with "Ahoy!"
Bell filed his telephone patent on the same day as rival inventor Elisha Gray.
Guglielmo Marconi sent the first radio signal across the Atlantic in 1901.
Philo Farnsworth came up with the idea for electronic television at age 14, while plowing straight rows in a potato field.
James Watt did not invent the steam engine, but he made it far more efficient. The unit of power, the watt, is named after him.
Benjamin Franklin invented bifocal glasses, the lightning rod and a better wood stove.
Franklin never patented his inventions, because he wanted everyone to be able to use them.
Leonardo da Vinci sketched designs for a parachute, a flying machine and an armored vehicle about 500 years ago.
In 2000, a skydiver tested a parachute built to Leonardo's design, and it worked.
Johannes Gutenberg's printing press, built around 1440, made books far faster and cheaper to produce.
Samuel Morse was a successful painter. He began working on the telegraph after slow mail kept him from reaching his dying wife in time.
Hollywood star Hedy Lamarr co-invented a frequency-hopping system in World War II, an idea later used in wireless technology.
King C. Gillette made shaving easier with the disposable razor blade in the early 1900s.
Levi Strauss and tailor Jacob Davis patented jeans with metal rivets in 1873.
Zippo lighters have been made in Bradford, Pennsylvania, since 1933, and come with a lifetime guarantee.
The Swiss Army knife was first supplied to Swiss soldiers in 1891.
The first product ever scanned with a barcode in a store was a pack of Wrigley's chewing gum in 1974.
Willis Carrier invented modern air conditioning in 1902 to control humidity in a printing plant, not to cool people.
Percy Shaw invented road reflectors called cat's eyes in 1934, after seeing a cat's eyes shine in his car's headlights.
The jet engine was invented separately and at about the same time by Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany.
James Dyson built 5,127 prototypes before his bagless vacuum cleaner worked.
Garrett Morgan, the son of formerly enslaved parents, patented an improved traffic signal and an early breathing hood.
Toothbrushes with nylon bristles first went on sale in 1938. Before that, bristles came from hogs.
Guitarist Les Paul built one of the first solid-body electric guitars in 1941. He called it The Log.
George Eastman's first Kodak camera in 1888 came loaded with film for 100 pictures. You mailed the whole camera back to get your photos.
Eli Whitney's cotton gin, invented in 1793, could clean cotton dozens of times faster than by hand.
The Wright brothers taught themselves engineering in their bicycle shop and had no university degrees.
