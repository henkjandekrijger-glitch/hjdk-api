## History's Strangest Moments
Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid of Giza.
Oxford University was already teaching students before the Aztecs founded their capital city, Tenochtitlan.
Woolly mammoths were still alive on a remote Arctic island when the Great Pyramid was built.
Napoleon was not unusually short. He was about 1.69 meters tall, roughly average for a Frenchman of his time.
Vikings did not wear horned helmets in battle. The idea became popular through opera costumes in the 1800s.
In 1518, dozens of people in Strasbourg danced for days without stopping, in an event known as the dancing plague.
The shortest war in history was fought between Britain and Zanzibar in 1896. It lasted under an hour.
In 1932, Australian soldiers with machine guns were sent to fight thousands of emus. The emus won the Great Emu War.
Ancient Egyptians often slept with their heads on curved headrests made of wood or stone instead of soft pillows.
Ancient Romans collected urine and used it to clean and whiten clothes.
Roman emperor Vespasian put a tax on collecting urine from public toilets. He is said to have replied that money does not stink.
The Great Fire of London in 1666 destroyed more than 13,000 houses, but only a handful of deaths were recorded.
The Eiffel Tower was built for the 1889 World's Fair and was only meant to stand for 20 years.
The Statue of Liberty was originally the color of a shiny copper penny. It turned green over about 30 years.
The Leaning Tower of Pisa began to lean while it was still being built in the 1170s, because of soft ground.
The Titanic had four funnels, but only three of them were working. The fourth was mostly for looks and ventilation.
Abraham Lincoln is in the National Wrestling Hall of Fame. He lost only one recorded match in about 300.
Before alarm clocks were common, workers in Britain paid knocker-uppers to tap on their windows with long poles.
One of the first photographs ever showing a person was taken in Paris in 1838. A man stood still long enough to have his boots polished.
US presidents John Adams and Thomas Jefferson both died on the same day: July 4, 1826, the 50th anniversary of independence.
Ancient Romans loved a strong fermented fish sauce called garum and put it on almost everything.
The Hundred Years' War between England and France actually lasted 116 years.
The Spanish flu of 1918 probably did not start in Spain. Spain's newspapers were simply free to report on it first.
When parts of Europe switched to the Gregorian calendar in 1582, October 4 was followed directly by October 15.
Britain and its colonies switched calendars in 1752, so September 2 was followed by September 14 that year.
Ancient Egyptians mummified millions of cats as offerings to the cat goddess Bastet.
For centuries, Europeans bought powder made from ground-up Egyptian mummies as medicine.
Coca-Cola originally contained small amounts of cocaine from coca leaves. It was removed in the early 1900s.
The first vending machine was described by Hero of Alexandria about 2,000 years ago. Drop in a coin and it gave out holy water.
Athletes in the ancient Olympic Games competed naked.
The Mongol Empire under Genghis Khan and his heirs was the largest connected land empire in history.
Tug of war was an Olympic sport from 1900 to 1920.
From 1912 to 1948, the Olympics also gave out medals for painting, music, sculpture, architecture and writing.
The Great Wall of China cannot be seen from the Moon with the naked eye. That is a popular myth.
Albert Einstein was offered the presidency of Israel in 1952. He politely turned it down.
The workers who built the Egyptian pyramids were most likely paid laborers, not slaves.
Ancient Romans had fast food. Street counters called thermopolia sold hot meals to people on the go.
One of the oldest known recipes is for beer, written in a Sumerian poem almost 4,000 years ago.
In 1859, a giant solar storm made telegraph wires spark, and northern lights were seen as far south as the Caribbean.
1816 was called the Year Without a Summer, after a huge volcanic eruption in Indonesia cooled the whole planet.
During that cold and gloomy summer of 1816, Mary Shelley began writing Frankenstein.
Queen Elizabeth II trained as a mechanic and military truck driver during World War II.
Purple was the color of emperors, because the dye was made from thousands of sea snails and cost a fortune.
In medieval Europe, animals were sometimes put on trial in court, including pigs, rats and insects.
The first passengers in a hot air balloon, in 1783, were a sheep, a duck and a rooster.
The Wright brothers' first powered flight in 1903 lasted just 12 seconds.
The fax machine was invented in 1843, more than 30 years before the telephone.
Nintendo was founded in 1889 as a company that made playing cards.
The Great Pyramid of Giza was the tallest human-made structure in the world for nearly 4,000 years.
During the Cold War, the US and the Soviet Union kept a direct hotline. It was a teletype link, not a red telephone.

## Science That Sounds Fake
Hot water can sometimes freeze faster than cold water. Scientists call this puzzle the Mpemba effect.
Glass is not a slow-moving liquid. Old windows are thicker at the bottom because of how they were made.
A bolt of lightning is about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun.
Lightning strikes somewhere on Earth around 40 to 50 times every second.
Lightning does strike the same place twice. The Empire State Building is hit about 20 to 25 times a year.
Bananas are slightly radioactive, because they contain potassium. You would need to eat millions at once for it to harm you.
At one special temperature and pressure, called the triple point, water can boil and freeze at the same time.
Helium is the only element that will not freeze solid at normal pressure, no matter how cold it gets.
Helium was discovered on the Sun in 1868, before anyone found it on Earth.
Diamonds and the graphite in your pencil are both made of pure carbon.
Atoms are almost entirely empty space. Squeeze it all out, and every human on Earth would fit into a space the size of a sugar cube.
Energy made in the Sun's core can take tens of thousands of years to reach the surface, then just over 8 minutes to reach Earth.
You have roughly as many bacteria in and on your body as human cells. The old claim of ten bacteria per human cell was wrong.
Water expands when it freezes. That is why ice floats and why frozen pipes can burst.
Because ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down, and fish can survive the winter in the water below.
A fluffy cumulus cloud can weigh around 500 tonnes, about as much as 100 elephants.
Some sand dunes sing. When sand slides down them, they can make a deep humming or booming sound.
Tardigrades, tiny animals also called water bears, have survived being exposed to the vacuum of space.
A dried-out tardigrade can come back to life when you add a drop of water.
The Eiffel Tower can grow up to about 15 centimeters taller in summer, because heat makes its iron expand.
The center of the Earth is about as hot as the surface of the Sun.
If you could fold a piece of paper in half 42 times, it would be thick enough to reach the Moon.
The metal gallium melts in your hand. It turns liquid at just under 30 degrees Celsius.
Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at ordinary room temperature.
Pure water is a poor conductor of electricity. It is the salts and minerals dissolved in tap water that carry the current.
When lightning strikes sand, it can melt it into hollow glass tubes called fulgurites.
Rainbows are actually full circles. From the ground, the horizon hides the lower part.
No two people ever see exactly the same rainbow, because each person sees light from different raindrops.
The sky is blue because air scatters blue sunlight much more than red light.
Sunsets are red and orange because the light passes through so much air that most of the blue is scattered away.
Snow is not really white. Each ice crystal is clear, but piles of them scatter all colors of light.
Bubbles are round because a sphere holds the most air with the least surface.
You can balance an egg on its end on any day of the year, not just on the spring equinox.
The spin of the Earth is far too weak to decide which way your sink or toilet drains.
An unprotected person in space would stay conscious for about 15 seconds. They would not instantly explode or freeze.
Light takes about 1.3 seconds to travel from the Moon to Earth.
Sound travels more than 15 times faster through steel than through air.
The eruption of Krakatoa in 1883 was heard nearly 5,000 kilometers away. It may be the loudest sound in recorded history.
One gram of gold can be hammered into a sheet about one square meter in size, so thin that light shines through it.
GPS satellites must correct for Einstein's relativity. Without it, your map position would drift by about 10 kilometers a day.
Time passes slightly faster on a mountaintop than at sea level, because gravity is a little weaker up there.
Tens of tons of space dust fall onto Earth every day.
Antarctica is the largest desert on Earth, because it gets so little rain or snow.
Water has no smell or taste. What you taste in water is the minerals and gases dissolved in it.
A teaspoon of soil can hold more living microbes than there are people on Earth.
Some frogs can freeze solid in winter, with no heartbeat, and thaw back to life in spring.
Every second, trillions of tiny particles called neutrinos from the Sun pass straight through your body without touching anything.
Very pure, still water can stay liquid well below 0 degrees Celsius. This is called supercooling.
Earth travels around the Sun at about 107,000 kilometers per hour, yet you cannot feel it at all.
Glass frogs have see-through skin on their bellies, so you can watch their hearts beat.

## Words & Language
The words set and run each have hundreds of different meanings in the Oxford English Dictionary.
The dot over a lowercase i or j is called a tittle.
The # symbol has an official-sounding old name: the octothorpe.
The ampersand, &, was once recited as the 27th letter of the English alphabet.
The word alphabet comes from alpha and beta, the first two letters of the Greek alphabet.
Strengths is one of the longest English words with only one vowel.
Rhythm is a common English word with none of the five standard vowels.
"The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" uses every letter of the alphabet. A sentence like that is called a pangram.
The longest word in major English dictionaries is pneumonoultramicroscopicsilicovolcanoconiosis, a lung disease. It has 45 letters.
Facetious and abstemious contain all five vowels in alphabetical order.
Goodbye is a shortened form of the old phrase "God be with you."
Quarantine comes from the Italian for forty days, the time ships had to wait before landing during plague outbreaks.
The word robot comes from a Czech word for forced labor. It was first used in a play by Karel Čapek in 1920.
The word nerd first appeared in the Dr. Seuss book If I Ran the Zoo in 1950.
Muscle comes from the Latin for little mouse, because a flexing muscle looks like a mouse moving under the skin.
Disaster comes from words meaning bad star, because people once blamed bad luck on the stars.
Window comes from an Old Norse word meaning wind eye.
Avocado comes from ahuacatl, a word in Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs.
Tomato and chili also come from Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs.
Ketchup probably comes from a word for fish sauce in a Chinese language spoken in southern China.
Jumbo became a word for anything huge thanks to a famous circus elephant named Jumbo in the 1880s.
Shampoo comes from a Hindi word meaning to press or massage.
Pajamas, jungle and bungalow all came into English from languages of India.
Mortgage literally means death pledge in Old French.
OK started as a joke abbreviation of oll korrect, a deliberate misspelling, in Boston newspapers in 1839.
A butt was once a real unit for wine, a large barrel holding about 477 liters.
Typewriter can be typed using only the top row of letters on a keyboard.
E is the most common letter in English.
Q and Z are usually the least used letters in English.
Mandarin Chinese has more native speakers than any other language.
English has the most speakers in total, when you count everyone who learned it as a second language.
Papua New Guinea has more than 800 living languages, more than any other country.
There are roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today.
About 40 percent of the world's languages are endangered.
On La Gomera in the Canary Islands, people still use Silbo Gomero, a whistled language that carries across valleys.
The Hawaiian alphabet has only 13 letters.
Khmer, the language of Cambodia, has one of the largest alphabets in the world, with 74 letters.
Rotokas, spoken in Papua New Guinea, has one of the smallest alphabets, with just 12 letters.
A palindrome reads the same forwards and backwards, like racecar or "Was it a car or a cat I saw?"
The word emoji comes from Japanese for picture plus character. It is not related to the English word emotion.
The Face with Tears of Joy emoji was chosen as Oxford Dictionaries' Word of the Year in 2015.
Shakespeare's plays contain the first recorded uses of many words, including bedroom and lonely.
Clue comes from clew, a ball of thread, from the Greek myth of Theseus following thread out of the labyrinth.
Sign languages are complete languages with their own grammar. American and British Sign Language are very different.
Every odd number written in English contains the letter e.
In American English, you can count all the way to one thousand before you need the letter a in a number's name.
Uncopyrightable is one of the longest English words in which no letter repeats.
Dreamt is the only common English word that ends in the letters mt.
Queue sounds exactly the same if you remove its last four letters.
Bookkeeper has three double letters in a row.

## Inventions & Happy Accidents
Penicillin was discovered by accident in 1928, when Alexander Fleming noticed mold killing bacteria in a dish he had left out.
The microwave oven was invented after engineer Percy Spencer noticed that radar equipment had melted a candy bar in his pocket.
Post-it Notes came from a failed attempt to make a super-strong glue at the company 3M.
Super Glue was discovered by accident during World War II, while researchers were trying to make clear plastic gun sights.
Velcro was inspired by burrs that stuck to a Swiss engineer's dog after a walk in the Alps.
Play-Doh was first sold as a wallpaper cleaner.
Bubble Wrap was first meant to be textured wallpaper.
The Slinky was invented when engineer Richard James watched a spring tumble off a shelf and keep moving.
The Popsicle was invented by 11-year-old Frank Epperson, who left a drink with a stirring stick outside on a cold night in 1905.
Saccharin, the first artificial sweetener, was discovered in 1879 when a chemist forgot to wash his hands before dinner.
X-rays were discovered by accident by Wilhelm Röntgen in 1895.
One of the first X-ray images ever taken showed the hand of Röntgen's wife, with her wedding ring clearly visible.
Teflon was discovered by accident in 1938 by a chemist trying to make a new gas for refrigerators.
The implantable pacemaker came about partly by accident, when inventor Wilson Greatbatch put the wrong part into a circuit.
Flaked cereal was discovered when the Kellogg brothers left cooked wheat out too long and then rolled it flat.
Chocolate chip cookies were invented around 1938 by Ruth Wakefield, who ran the Toll House Inn in Massachusetts.
Coca-Cola was invented by a pharmacist, John Pemberton, in 1886 and was first sold as a tonic.
Pepsi was originally called Brad's Drink, after its inventor Caleb Bradham.
In 1947, engineers found a real moth stuck inside a Harvard computer and taped it into their logbook as the first actual bug.
The first webcam watched a coffee pot at Cambridge University, so researchers could check if it was empty without walking there.
The first item ever sold on eBay was a broken laser pointer. It sold for about $14.
The first YouTube video, "Me at the zoo," was uploaded in 2005 and is just 19 seconds long.
The first computer mouse, built in the 1960s, was made of wood.
The original patent for the fire hydrant was destroyed in a fire at the US Patent Office in 1836.
The safety pin was invented in 1849 by Walter Hunt, who sold the patent for $400 to pay off a debt.
Windshield wipers were invented by Mary Anderson in 1903, after she watched a streetcar driver struggle to see in the snow.
Kevlar, the material in bulletproof vests, was invented by chemist Stephanie Kwolek in 1965.
The first successful dishwasher was patented by Josephine Cochrane in 1886. She wanted a machine that would not chip her fine china.
Monopoly is based on The Landlord's Game, created by Lizzie Magie to show the problems with monopolies.
Earmuffs were invented by 15-year-old Chester Greenwood in 1873, because his ears got cold while ice skating.
The trampoline was invented by George Nissen, who was inspired by trapeze artists bouncing off their safety nets.
The ice cream cone became famous at the 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis.
Thomas Edison did not invent the first light bulb. He made a practical bulb that lasted long enough to be useful.
The first person convicted of speeding, in England in 1896, was driving at about 13 kilometers per hour.
Under a British law from 1865, early motor vehicles had to be led by a person walking ahead with a red flag.
The Frisbee got its name from the Frisbie Pie Company, whose empty pie tins students liked to throw.
Silly Putty was an accident from World War II, when scientists were trying to make a rubber substitute.
The stethoscope was invented in 1816 by doctor René Laennec, who felt awkward putting his ear directly on a patient's chest.
The Rubik's Cube was invented in 1974 by Ernő Rubik, a Hungarian architecture professor, as a teaching tool.
A Rubik's Cube has more than 43 quintillion possible positions.
Instant noodles were invented in 1958 by Momofuku Ando in Japan.
Momofuku Ando later helped create instant noodles that astronauts could eat in space.
The first traffic light was installed in London in 1868, before cars existed. It was gas-powered and exploded a few weeks later.
Lego bricks made in 1958 still click together with Lego bricks made today.
By number of tires made, Lego has been called the world's largest tire maker, thanks to all its tiny toy wheels.
Barbie's full name is Barbara Millicent Roberts.
Mr. Potato Head was one of the first toys ever advertised on television, in 1952.
The modern bicycle with two equal wheels and a chain drive appeared in the 1880s. Before that, the penny-farthing had one giant wheel.
The first rollercoasters grew out of ice slides that were popular in Russia in the 1600s.
The can opener was invented about 50 years after the tin can. Early cans were opened with a hammer and chisel.

## Money
US dollar bills are not made of paper. They are a blend of 75 percent cotton and 25 percent linen.
A US dollar bill can be folded back and forth about 4,000 times before it tears.
An average US $1 bill stays in circulation for about six to seven years.
In 2025, the United States stopped making new pennies for circulation, because each one cost almost 4 cents to produce.
Canada stopped making pennies in 2012.
The first coins were made around 2,600 years ago in Lydia, in what is now Turkey.
China was the first country to use paper money, more than 1,000 years ago.
The British pound is often called the oldest currency still in use today.
On the island of Yap in the Pacific, giant stone discs up to about 3.6 meters wide were used as money.
One Yap stone coin is said to lie at the bottom of the sea, but it still counts, because everyone agrees who owns it.
Cows, shells, salt and bricks of tea have all been used as money at different times in history.
Cowrie shells were used as money in parts of Africa and Asia for centuries.
Calling a dollar a buck may come from deerskins, which were traded like money in colonial America.
The dollar sign probably comes from an old abbreviation for the Spanish peso.
The largest US banknote ever printed was the $100,000 bill. It was only used for transfers between banks.
Woodrow Wilson is the face on that $100,000 bill.
In 2021, El Salvador became the first country to make Bitcoin official money.
In 2010, a programmer paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas. That amount of bitcoin later became worth over a billion dollars.
May 22 is celebrated by Bitcoin fans as Bitcoin Pizza Day.
In Hungary in 1946, prices doubled about every 15 hours, the worst hyperinflation ever recorded.
Zimbabwe once printed a 100 trillion dollar banknote.
During German hyperinflation in 1923, money became so worthless that people burned banknotes to keep warm.
Sweden is one of the most cashless countries in the world.
Mansa Musa, a king of Mali in the 1300s, is often called the richest person who ever lived.
Mansa Musa handed out so much gold on his journey to Mecca in 1324 that the price of gold in Cairo dropped for years.
The first ATM opened in London in 1967.
The PIN code for the first ATM was planned to be six digits, but the inventor's wife said she could only remember four.
The bridges and windows on euro banknotes are invented, so no country's real landmarks are favored.
Australia was the first country to issue plastic banknotes, in 1988.
Plastic banknotes can survive a trip through the washing machine.
Benjamin Franklin appears on the $100 bill, but he was never president.
Alexander Hamilton on the $10 bill was never president either.
Martha Washington is the only woman whose portrait has been the main image on a US paper banknote.
The British Guiana 1c Magenta, the world's most valuable stamp, sold for about $8.3 million in 2021.
A 1933 Double Eagle gold coin sold for about $18.9 million in 2021, a record price for a coin.
Leonardo da Vinci's Salvator Mundi sold for $450 million in 2017, the most ever paid for a painting.
A banana taped to a wall, an artwork called Comedian, sold for $6.2 million in 2024. The buyer later ate the banana.
If you start with one cent and double it every day, by day 30 you would have more than $5 million.
During World War II, special Monopoly sets were used to smuggle real maps, compasses and money to prisoners of war.
Fort Knox in Kentucky holds about half of the US government's gold.
A standard gold bar in a central bank vault weighs about 12.4 kilograms.
Prices ending in .99 work because shoppers focus on the first digit, so $9.99 feels much cheaper than $10.
In Scotland, three different banks still print their own banknotes.
The US Secret Service was created in 1865 to fight fake money, not to protect presidents.
The 1,000 Swiss franc note is one of the most valuable banknotes still printed today.
In Kenya, millions of people send and receive money with simple text messages through a service called M-Pesa.
The first banknotes in Europe were issued in Sweden in 1661.
A stack of 100 US banknotes is only about 1 centimeter thick.
The word money comes from Juno Moneta, a Roman goddess whose temple housed the city's mint.
Sweets, cigarettes, and even packets of instant noodles have been used as money inside prisons.
